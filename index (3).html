The story behind the technical

Every engineering decision is also a business decision: what it costs, how long it runs, and whether it can be built again.

I learned engineering on the shop floor: testing crude in a refinery lab, tuning acid regeneration at a steel mill and running brewing operations, before graduating with First Class Honours, second in my class overall and first in the final year.

As a process engineer at Fullcare, one of East Africa’s largest medical device manufacturers, I helped qualify a packaging line through installation, operational and performance qualification (IQ, OQ, PQ). That taught me that a line only creates value once it is proven to make the right product, every time, to a regulator’s standard.

I then joined the team at Octavia Carbon, building the first direct air capture plant in the Global South, where we were turning a nascent technology into a plant that runs around the clock. With no playbook to follow, I owned process performance across the site: commissioning new systems, cutting energy and water use, preventing repeat trips, and building the measurement behind every tonne the business reports.

The thread through all of it is the same: process engineering that pays for itself in lower running costs, higher uptime and faster, safer delivery.

What it means for a business

The technical work, translated into what managers, investors and customers care about.

  • 01

    Lower operating cost

    Energy, water and chemicals are a plant’s biggest running costs. I find savings in how equipment is operated before anyone spends on new kit, such as a 26–28% energy advantage from changing boiler operation and a modelled reduction of over a third in water intensity through condensate recovery.

  • 02

    Higher uptime

    Every unplanned stop is lost output. I trace recurring faults to their root cause and design them out, including safety interlocks that removed two recurring categories of process trip.

  • 03

    Design for manufacture and scale

    A first unit is a prototype; the tenth should be cheaper and faster to build. I favour standard, serviceable equipment, benchmark suppliers before selecting (six vendors on one recent blower decision), and document designs so they can be repeated.

  • 04

    Projects delivered safely and on plan

    I plan and run staged commissioning with pre-startup safety reviews, so new systems start up without surprises. I’ve built commissioning frameworks across multiple subsystems and led design reviews with mechanical, automation and operations teams.

  • 05

    Quality that stands up to audit

    From IQ, OQ and PQ on a medical device packaging line to the uncertainty analysis behind reported production, I make sure results can be trusted by customers, certifiers and regulators.

  • 06

    Safe, compliant operations

    As Chemical Safety Officer I wrote chemical-handling procedures adopted across the site and run regular toolbox talks, with zero recordable incidents.

    In R&D I designed and built the platforms the team used to test materials and processes, from bench-top analyzers to larger rigs that came before scale-up.

    Micro-scale testing

    Breakthrough analyzer

    A compact bench-top analyzer that measures how quickly small material samples take up gas until they are saturated, known as breakthrough. Three switchable sample columns, mass flow control, and humidity and gas-concentration measurement on a single panel.

    Why it matters: materials can be screened quickly on small samples, before money is committed to larger builds.

    2. 2. Instruments fitted

    Bench to pilot scale

    Cyclic test rig

    An instrumented rig that runs full contactor sections through repeated capture-and-release cycles, with temperature, pressure and gas sensing along the flow path.

    Why it matters: it shows how performance holds up over many cycles, the evidence needed to set replacement intervals and design the next unit.

    Before scale-up

    Larger process rig

    A larger unit with automated valves and condensate handling, used to prove process steps closer to plant scale.

    Why it matters: it finds problems while they are still cheap to fix.

    From bench to field

    Full-size units on site

    Where the rigs lead: full-size units running outdoors, built on what the bench and pilot tests proved.

    Why it matters: each step up in scale was backed by data, not guesswork.
